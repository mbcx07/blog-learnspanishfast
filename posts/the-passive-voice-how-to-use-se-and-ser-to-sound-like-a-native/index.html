The Passive Voice: How to Use 'Se' and 'Ser' to Sound Like a Native

2026-05-18 · Grammar · Tags: The, grammar, learnspanishfast

When you first start learning Spanish, you're taught to build sentences with a clear subject performing an action: Juan come la manzana (Juan eats the apple). But as you advance, you'll notice that native speakers often flip the script. They'll say Se come la manzana or La manzana es comida—both meaning roughly "the apple is eaten." This is the passive voice, and mastering it is a game-changer for your fluency. Not only does it make your Spanish sound more natural, but it also helps you express ideas without always pointing fingers at who did what. In this guide, we'll break down the two main ways to form the passive voice in Spanish—using ser and using the impersonal se—and give you the tools to choose between them like a pro.

What Is the Passive Voice and Why Does It Matter?

The passive voice shifts the focus from the doer of an action to the receiver of that action. In English, we say "The cake was baked by Maria" instead of "Maria baked the cake." In Spanish, you have two primary ways to achieve this: the passive with ser (which mirrors the English structure) and the impersonal se (which is more common in everyday speech). Understanding both is crucial because they're not interchangeable in every context. The passive voice allows you to be more objective, to describe processes, and to talk about events without specifying who performed them—a skill that's essential for news reports, instructions, and casual conversation alike.

The Passive with Ser: The Direct Equivalent

The passive with ser is the closest to English. You conjugate ser in the same tense as the main verb of the active sentence, and then add the past participle of the action verb, which must agree in gender and number with the subject. For example:

  • La carta es escrita por Juan. (The letter is written by Juan.)
  • Las cartas son escritas por Juan. (The letters are written by Juan.)
  • La carta fue escrita por Juan. (The letter was written by Juan.)

Notice how the participle escrito changes to escrita to match the feminine singular carta, and escritas for plural. This construction is common in formal writing, journalism, and when you want to emphasize the action itself rather than the agent. However, in everyday spoken Spanish, you'll rarely hear it—it can sound stiff or overly formal. That's where the impersonal se comes in.

The Impersonal Se: The Everyday Workhorse

If you want to sound like a native, you need to embrace the impersonal se. This construction is used to express passive ideas without specifying who does the action. It's formed by placing se before the verb, which is conjugated in the third person singular or plural depending on the object. For example:

  • Se habla español aquí. (Spanish is spoken here.)
  • Se venden casas. (Houses are sold.)
  • Se comió toda la pizza. (All the pizza was eaten.)

The key is that the verb agrees with the object: if the object is singular, use the singular form; if plural, use plural. This is often called the pasiva refleja (reflexive passive). It's incredibly versatile and used in all types of contexts—from signs and instructions to news and casual chat. For instance, you'll see Se prohíbe fumar (Smoking is prohibited) or Se buscan actores (Actors are sought). The impersonal se is your go-to for most everyday situations.

Choosing Between Ser and Se: When to Use Which

So how do you decide? The general rule is: use ser when you want to emphasize the action and the agent is either important or explicitly mentioned. Use se when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or you want to sound more natural. Here are some guidelines:

  • Use ser for formal or written contexts—like academic papers, news reports, or legal documents. Example: El presidente fue elegido por el pueblo. (The president was elected by the people.)
  • Use se for general statements and instructions—like signs, recipes, or everyday conversation. Example: Se necesitan empleados. (Employees are needed.)
  • Use se when the agent is obvious or unimportant—like Se habla español (Spanish is spoken) or Se alquila apartamento (Apartment for rent).
  • Use ser when you want to sound more precise or dramatic—like in storytelling or when the agent is a key part of the sentence. Example: La casa fue construida por mi abuelo. (The house was built by my grandfather.)

In many cases, both are grammatically correct, but the nuance changes. For instance, Se vendió el coche (The car was sold) sounds neutral, while El coche fue vendido por el concesionario (The car was sold by the dealership) sounds more formal and specific. As a learner, start with se for everyday speech and reserve ser for when you need to be explicit about the doer.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Even advanced learners stumble on a few passive voice traps. Here are the most frequent mistakes and how to fix them:

  • Mixing up the participle agreement: With ser, the past participle must agree with the subject in gender and number. Don't say La carta es escrito—it's escrita. With se, the verb agrees with the object, not the subject. So Se vende casas is wrong; it should be Se venden casas.
  • Using se with a specific agent: You can't say Se vende la casa por Juan to mean "the house is sold by Juan." The impersonal se doesn't allow an agent. If you need to mention the doer, switch to ser.
  • Overusing the passive voice: Spanish, like English, prefers active voice in many contexts. Don't force the passive when the active is clearer. For example, instead of La manzana es comida por mí, just say Yo como la manzana.
  • Confusing se with reflexive verbs: Not every se is passive. Se lava can mean "he washes himself" (reflexive) or "it is washed" (passive) depending on context. Pay attention to the object and the meaning.

To avoid these, practice by converting active sentences to both passive forms and notice the differences. Also, listen to native speakers—podcasts, news, and TV shows are goldmines for hearing how the passive voice is used naturally.

Practice Strategies to Make the Passive Voice Second Nature

Now that you understand the theory, it's time to internalize it. Here are some actionable exercises you can do today:

  • Rewrite news headlines: Take an active headline like "El gobierno aprueba la ley" and rewrite it in both passive forms: La ley es aprobada por el gobierno and Se aprueba la ley. Notice how the tone changes.
  • Describe processes: Write a short recipe or set of instructions using only se. For example, Se cortan las cebollas, se fríen en aceite, se añade sal. This forces you to use the impersonal passive naturally.
  • Listen and repeat: Find a Spanish podcast or news segment and pause after each sentence that uses se or ser in the passive. Repeat it aloud, mimicking the intonation. This builds muscle memory.
  • Create your own examples: Think of everyday situations—like at a restaurant, in a store, or at work—and write down passive sentences you might hear. For instance, Se sirve desayuno hasta las 11 (Breakfast is served until 11) or Se aceptan tarjetas de crédito (Credit cards are accepted).

Remember, the goal isn't to use the passive voice all the time—it's to have it in your toolkit so you can choose the most natural expression. As you practice, you'll develop an ear for when se flows better than ser, and vice versa.

Conclusion

The passive voice in Spanish might seem like a minor grammar point, but it's actually a gateway to sounding more fluent and native. By mastering both the ser passive and the impersonal se, you'll be able to express yourself with greater nuance, handle formal and informal situations with ease, and understand native speakers who use these constructions constantly. Start by incorporating se into your daily practice—it's the quickest way to sound natural. Then, as you become comfortable, experiment with ser in more formal contexts. With time and consistent practice, you'll find that the passive voice becomes second nature, and your Spanish will take a giant leap forward. So go ahead—se dice that you're on your way to mastery!
